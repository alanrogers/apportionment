\title{Small $F_{ST}$ Does Not Imply Small Population Differences in
  Quantitative Characters}
\author{Alan R. Rogers}
\maketitle

In a celebrated paper, \citet{Lewontin:EB-6-381} showed that
differences between the major human populations account for only a
small fraction of human genetic variation. On this basis, he concluded
that these differences are of no biological importance. This note will
argue that this conclusion is unwarranted.  Lewontin's argument can be
criticized in several ways. First, \citet{Edwards:BE-25-798} observed
that population differences become more informative when one considers
the pattern of correlations among populations---a pattern that
Lewontin ignored. Second, Lewontin himself pointed out that the loci
contributing to a particular character may differ more between
populations than predicted from the genome-wide average
\citep{Lewontin+Krakauer:73}, an idea that is often used in recent
literature \citep[e.g.\ ][]{Norton:MBE-24-710}. But my point is
different than either of these. I will argue that the values of a
quantitative character in two populations may differ by many standard
deviations even if there is little between-group variance at the
underlying loci. Consequently, Lewontin's observation does not imply
that genetic population differences must small for human quantitative
characters.

My thesis reflects the fact---demonstrated below---that the difference
between character means scales with the number, $K$, of loci, whereas
the standard deviation scales with $\sqrt{K}$. If $K$ is large, the
difference may amount to many standard deviations. The same idea
underlies a well-established model of limits to selection on a
quantitative character \citep[pp.~218--219]{Falconer:IQG-96}.

To see how this principle applies to Lewontin's problem, consider a
set of $K$ genetic loci, which vary within two populations of equal
size. At each locus there are two alleles, called ``plus'' and
``minus'' for reasons explained below. The frequency of each plus
allele is $p_1 = 1/2 + \delta$ in population~1, $p_2 = 1/2 - \delta$
in population~2, and $\bar p = 1/2$ in the population as a whole. Let
us use this model to calculate Wright's $F_{ST}$, which measures the
proportional reduction in heterozygosity caused by differences among
populations. It is by definition $F_{ST} = B\big/\{\bar p(1-\bar p)\}$,
where $B$ is the between-group component of variance in allele
frequencies \citep{Wright:AE-15-323}. For our model, $B = (p_1 -
1/2)^2/2 + (p_2 - 1/2)^2/2 = \delta^2$. This implies that
\[
  F_{ST} = 4\delta^2
\]
Although Lewontin did not use $F_{ST}$, we can paraphrase his main
result by saying that $F_{ST}$ among the major human populations is
small. To make a point, I will assume that $F_{ST}$ is even smaller
than the values typically estimated from human data.

Given that $F_{ST}$ is small, what does this imply about group
differences in a quantitative character controlled by these same loci?
I assume that, at each locus, the plus allele adds one unit to
character value, and the minus allele subtracts one unit. The
environment is the same in both groups and therefore makes no
contribution to the difference between groups.  With this setup, each locus
contributes $4\delta$ to the mean character value in population~1 and
$-4\delta$ to that in population~2. Each locus thus contributes
$8\delta$ to the difference between populations. Summing across loci,
\[
D = 8K\delta
\]
is the difference in mean character value between the populations.

Environmental effects become important as we consider variance within
groups. Heritability within each population is by definition $h^2 =
V_G/(V_G + V_E)$, where $V_G$ and $V_E$ are the additive genetic and
environmental components of variance. The total variance within groups
can thus be written as $V_G + V_E = V_G h^{-2}$.  The additive genetic
variance contributed by a single locus within population~$i$ is
$2p_i(1-p_i)\alpha^2$, where $\alpha=2$ is the average effect of a
gene substitution \citep{Fisher:AE-11-53}. For either population, this
equals $2(1 - 4\delta^2)$. Summing across loci,
\[
V_G = 2K(1 - 4\delta^2)
\]
The variance here is a sum of per-locus contributions, because I have
assumed linkage equilibrium within each population between all pairs
of loci.

The mean difference between the character values of the two
populations, expressed in units of the within-population standard
deviation, is
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \frac{D}{\sqrt{V_G + V_E}} &=&
  \frac{8K\delta}{\sqrt{h^{-2}2K(1 - 4\delta^2)}}\\
  &\approx& 5.7 h\sqrt{K}\delta
\end{eqnarray*}
where the approximation is good if $\delta^2$ is small.  To make this
concrete, let us assume that $\delta=0.1$ so that $F_{ST} = 0.04$---a
value somewhat smaller than that of the continental human
populations. If heritability is $h^2=0.5$, and the number of loci is
$K=100$, then the two populations differ by 4 standard
deviations. Fig.~\ref{fig.4sigma} shows what this would look like. The
two distributions are well separated, with only minor overlap. If
instead there were 1000 loci, the difference would exceed 12 standard
deviations.

\begin{figure}
  {\centering\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{4sigma.pdf}\\}
  \caption{Two normal distributions with means 4 standard deviations
    apart.}
  \label{fig.4sigma}
\end{figure}

These results show that small differences in allele frequencies can
cause large differences in a quantitative character.  A small value of
$F_{ST}$ does not preclude substantial genetic differences between
populations.
