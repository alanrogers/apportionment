It has been known since the early 1970s that differences between
continental human populations account for only a small fraction of
total genetic variation \citep{Lewontin:EB-6-381, Nei:S-177-434,
  Nei:AJH-26-421}.  On this basis, Richard Lewontin concluded that
``It is clear that our perception of relatively large differences
between human races and subgroups, as compared to the variation within
these groups, is indeed a biased perception and that, based on
randomly chosen genetic differences, human races and populations are
remarkably similar to each other''
\citep[p.~397]{Lewontin:EB-6-381}. In the half-century since these
papers appeared, this interpretation has been criticized
\citep{Spielman:AJH-28-317, Mitton:AN-111-203, Edwards:BE-25-798} and
defended \citep{Roseman:BE-43-2100204, Shen:PRS-377-20200417,
  Hochman:RRG-22}. It is also sometimes broadened into a stronger
claim: that when the between-group component of genetic variance is
small, there can be no important genetic difference between the
groups. This must be true in the limiting case when there are no
genetic differences at all. But what if these differences are merely
small? How much does this constrain group differences in, say, a
quantitative character?

One answer to this question was suggested by Lewontin himself, who
pointed out that the loci contributing to a particular character may
differ more between populations than the average locus does
\citep{Lewontin+Krakauer:73}, an idea that has often been used to look
for evidence of selection
\parencites[e.g.{}][]{Rogers+Harpending:QuantitativeCharacters}
           {Norton:MBE-24-710}. This note will provide an additional
           answer, which focuses on the loci contributing to the
           character. If \emph{these} loci vary only slightly among
           groups, how much does this constrain group differences in
           the character they control?

I will show that this constraint is weak if the number of loci is
large. This reflects the fact---demonstrated below---that the
difference between character means scales with $K$, the number of
loci, whereas the within-group standard deviation of character value
scales with $\sqrt{K}$. If $K$ is large, the difference between groups
may amount to many standard deviations. The same idea underlies a
well-established model of limits to selection on a quantitative
character \citep[pp.~218--219]{Falconer:IQG-96}.

The present model will involve $K$ genetic loci in two populations of
equal size. Each locus has two alleles called ``plus'' and ``minus''
for reasons explained below. The frequency of each plus allele is $p_1
= 1/2 + \delta$ in population~1, $p_2 = 1/2 - \delta$ in population~2,
and $\bar p = 1/2$ in the population as a whole. The between-group
component of variance in allele frequencies $B = (p_1 - \bar p)^2/2 +
(p_2 - \bar p)^2/2 = \delta^2$. Let us use this model to calculate
Wright's $F_{ST}$, which measures the proportional reduction in
heterozygosity caused by differences among populations. By definition,
$F_{ST} = B\big/\{\bar p(1-\bar p)\}$ \citep{Wright:AE-15-323}, which
reduces under our model to
\[
F_{ST} = 4\delta^2
\]
The question now becomes, if $F_{ST}$ is small for a set of loci, how
much does this constrain the difference between groups in a
quantitative character they control?

I assume that each copy of the plus allele adds one unit to character
value, and each minus adds zero. The environment is the same in both
groups and therefore makes no contribution to the difference between
groups.  With this setup, each locus contributes $1+2\delta$ to the mean
character value in population~1, $1-2\delta$ to that in population~2,
and $4\delta$ to the difference between populations. Summing across
loci,
\[
D = 4K\delta
\]
is the difference in mean character value between the populations.

Within each group, heritability is by definition $h^2 = V_G/(V_G +
V_E)$, where $V_G$ and $V_E$ are the additive genetic and
environmental components of variance. The total variance within groups
can thus be written as $V_G + V_E = V_G h^{-2}$.  The additive genetic
variance contributed by a single locus within population~$i$ is
$2p_i(1-p_i)\alpha^2$, where $\alpha$ is the average effect of a gene
substitution \citep{Fisher:AE-11-53} and equals 1 for this model. For
either population, this equals $(1 - 4\delta^2)/2$. Summing across
loci,
\[
V_G = K(1 - 4\delta^2)/2
\]
The variance here is a sum of per-locus contributions, because I have
assumed linkage equilibrium within each population between all pairs
of loci.

The mean difference between the character values of the two
populations, expressed in units of the within-population standard
deviation, is
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \frac{D}{\sqrt{V_G + V_E}} &=&
  \frac{4K\delta}{\sqrt{h^{-2}K(1 - 4\delta^2)/2}}\\
  &\approx& 5.7 h\sqrt{K}\delta
\end{eqnarray*}
where the approximation is good if $\delta^2$ is small. Similar
expressions have been derived before
\parencites[p.~799]{Edwards:BE-25-798}[before Eqn.~5]{Edge:SHP-52-32},
but those authors were concerned with classification rather than with
how $F_{ST}$ constrains quantitative characters.

To make this result concrete, let us assume that $\delta=0.1$ so that
$F_{ST} = 0.04$---a value somewhat smaller than that of the
continental human populations. If heritability is $h^2=0.5$, and the
number of loci is $K=100$, then the two populations differ by 4
standard deviations. Fig.~\ref{fig.4sigma} shows what this would look
like. The two distributions are well separated, with only minor
overlap. If instead there were 1000 loci, the difference would exceed
12 standard deviations.

Under neutral evolution, the expected variance among groups in a
quantitative character is proportional to $F_{ST}$
\citep{Rogers+Harpending:QuantitativeCharacters}, and many studies
have compared observed variation to this neutral expectation. It
usually turns out that quantitative characters vary more than a
neutral model would predict, suggesting either natural selection or
environmental heterogeneity \citep{Leinonen:JEB-21-1}.  The present
results show that the genetic component of this excess may be very
large.  Small differences in allele frequencies can cause large
differences in a quantitative character.  Large group differences in a
quantitative character do not require large allele frequency
differences at underlying loci.
