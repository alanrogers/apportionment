\title{The Misapportionment of Human Diversity}
\author{Alan R. Rogers}
\maketitle

In a celebrated paper, \citet{Lewontin:EB-6-381} showed that
differences between the major human populations account for only a
small fraction of human genetic variation. On this basis, he concluded
that these differences are of no biological importance. This note will
argue that this conclusion is unwarranted.

There are two reasons for skepticism. First, the the loci contributing
to a particular character may differ more between populations than
predicted from the genome-wide average---an idea first suggested by
Lewontin himself \citep{Lewontin+Krakauer:73} and later implemented by
others \citep[e.g.\ ][]{Norton:MBE-24-710}. Second, the values of a
polygenic character in two populations may differ by many standard
deviations even if there is little between-group variance at the
underlying loci. For both reasons, Lewontin's observation does not
imply that genetic population differences must small for human
polygenic characters.

The second of these objections reflects the fact---demonstrated
below---that the difference between character means scales with the
number, $K$, of loci, whereas the standard deviation scales with
$\sqrt{K}$. If $K$ is large, the difference may amount to many
standard deviations. The same idea underlies a well-established model
of limits to selection on a polygenic character
\citep[pp.~218--219]{Falconer:IQG-96}.

To see how this principle applies to Lewontin's problem, consider a
set of $K$ genetic loci, which vary within two populations of equal
size. At each locus there are two alleles, called ``plus'' and
``minus'' for reasons explained below. The frequency of each plus
allele is $p_1 = 1/2 + \delta$ in population~1, $p_2 = 1/2 - \delta$
in population~2, and $1/2$ in the population as a whole.  The
between-group component of variance in allele frequencies is $B = (p_1
- 1/2)^2/2 + (p_2 - 1/2)^2/2 = \delta^2$. The within-group component
is $W = 2p_1(1-p_1)/2 + 2p_2(1-p_2)/2 = 1/2 - 2\delta^2$. This implies
that $F_{ST}$, which measures the between-group variance in allele
frequencies as a fraction of total genetic variance, is equal to
\begin{eqnarray*}
  F_{ST} &=& B/(B+W)\\
  &=& \frac{\delta^2}{\delta^2 + 1/2 - 2\delta^2}\\
  &\approx& 2\delta^2
\end{eqnarray*}
where the approximation assumes that $\delta^2$ is small. Although
Lewontin did not use $F_{ST}$, we can paraphrase his main result by
saying that $F_{ST}$ among the major human populations is small. To
make a point, I will assume that $F_{ST}$ is even smaller than the
values typically estimated from human data.

Given that $F_{ST}$ is small, what does this imply about group
differences in a polygenic character controlled by these same loci? I
assume that, at each locus, the plus allele adds one unit to character
value, and the minus allele subtracts one unit. The environment is the
same in both groups and therefore makes no contribution to
between-group variance.  With this setup, each locus contributes
$4\delta$ to the mean character value in population~1 and $-4\delta$
to that in population~2. Each locus thus contributes $8\delta$ to the
difference between populations. Summing across loci,
\[
D = 8K\delta
\]
is the difference in mean character value between the populations.

Environmental effects become important as we turn to the within-group
component of variance. The heritability within each population is by
definition $h^2 = V_G/(V_G + V_E)$, where $V_G$ and $V_E$ are the
additive genetic and environmental components of variance. The total
variance within groups can thus be written as $V_G + V_E = V_G
h^{-2}$.  The additive genetic variance contributed by a single locus
within population~$i$ is $2p_i(1-p_i)\alpha^2$, where $\alpha=2$ is
the average effect of a gene substitution \citep{Fisher:AE-11-53}. For
either population, this equals $2(1 - 4\delta^2)$. Summing across
loci,
\[
V_G = 2K(1 - 4\delta^2)
\]
The variance here is a sum of per-locus contributions, because I have
assumed linkage equilibrium within each population between all pairs
of loci.

The mean difference between the character values of the two
populations, expressed in units of the within-population standard
deviation, is
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \frac{D}{\sqrt{V_G + V_E}} &=&
  \frac{8K\delta}{\sqrt{h^{-2}2K(1 - 4\delta^2)}}\\
  &\approx& 5.7 h\sqrt{K}\delta
\end{eqnarray*}
To make this concrete, let us assume that $\delta=0.1$ so that
$F_{ST}\approx 0.02$---a value much smaller than that of the
continental human populations. If heritability is $h^2=0.5$, and the
number of loci is $K=100$, then the two populations differ by 4
standard deviations. Fig.~\ref{fig.4sigma} shows what this would look
like. The two distributions are well separated, with only minor
overlap. If instead there are 1000 loci, the difference exceeds 12
standard deviations. This shows that small differences in allele
frequencies can cause large differences in a quantitative character.

  \caption{Two normal distributions with means 4 standard deviations
    apart.}
  \label{fig.4sigma}
\end{figure}

Lewontin expressed his conclusion as a critique of the concept of
race, and I have no wish to promote this concept. But in discussing
such issues, it is important to understand that a small value of
$F_{ST}$ does not exclude the possibility of important genetic
differences between populations.
