\title{Large Genetic Group Differences in Spite of Small Differences
  in Allele Frequencies}
\author{Alan R. Rogers}
\maketitle

It has been known since the early 1970s that at the average genetic
locus, variation among continental human populations is much smaller
than variation within them \citep{Lewontin:EB-6-381, Nei:S-177-434,
  Nei:AJH-26-421}.  On this basis, \citet[p.~127]{Lewontin:NOG-84}
concluded that ``Any use of racial categories must take its
justifications from some other source than biology.'' Although this
view has provoked debate \citep{Spielman:AJH-28-317,
  Mitton:AN-111-203, Edwards:BE-25-798, Crow:D-131-81,
  Roseman:BE-43-2100204, Shen:PRS-377-20200417, Hochman:RRG-22}, it is
widely interpreted to mean that when two populations have similar
allele frequencies, there can be no important genetic difference
between them. This note will not address the issue of race. Instead,
it will ask a basic question. If groups have similar allele
frequencies, how does this constrain genetic group differences in a
quantitative character?

One answer to this question was suggested by Lewontin himself, who
pointed out that the loci contributing to a particular character may
differ more between populations than the average locus does
\citep{Lewontin+Krakauer:73}, an idea that has often been used to look
for evidence of selection
\parencites[e.g.{}][]{Rogers+Harpending:QuantitativeCharacters}{Norton:MBE-24-710}. In
many species (including humans) group differences are large in a few
genomic regions in spite of low differentiation across most of the
genome \citep{Akey:GR-12-1805, Turner:PLO-3-e285,
  Cruickshank:ME-23-3133, Nosil:PRS-279-5058}. The difference between
groups could clearly be large for a quantitative character controlled
by these differentiated regions. But what of characters controlled by
other genomic regions, in which the groups have similar allele
frequencies?

This note will show that group differences in a quantitative character
may be large even if differences are small at the loci that affect the
character. This reflects the fact---demonstrated below---that the
difference between character means scales with $K$, the number of
loci, whereas the within-group standard deviation scales with
$\sqrt{K}$. If $K$ is large, the difference between groups may amount
to many standard deviations.

The model involves $K$ genetic loci in two populations of equal
size. Each locus has two alleles called ``plus'' and ``minus.'' The
frequency of each plus allele is $p_1 = 1/2 + \delta$ in population~1,
$p_2 = 1/2 - \delta$ in population~2, and $\bar p = 1/2$ in the
population as a whole. The between-group component of variance in
allele frequencies $B = (p_1 - \bar p)^2/2 + (p_2 - \bar p)^2/2 =
\delta^2$. Let us use this model to calculate Wright's $F_{ST}$, which
measures the proportional reduction in heterozygosity caused by
differences among populations. By definition, $F_{ST} = B\big/\{\bar
p(1-\bar p)\}$ \citep{Wright:AE-15-323}, which reduces under our model
to
\begin{equation}
  F_{ST} = 4\delta^2
  \label{eq.Fst}
\end{equation}
The question now becomes, if $F_{ST}$ is small for a set of loci, how
much does this constrain the difference between groups in a
quantitative character they control?

I assume that each copy of the plus allele adds one unit to character
value, and each minus adds zero. The environment is the same in both
groups and therefore makes no contribution to the difference between
them.  With this setup, each locus contributes $1+2\delta$ to the mean
character value in population~1, $1-2\delta$ to that in population~2,
and $4\delta$ to the difference between populations. Summing across
loci, the difference in mean character value is
\[
D = 4K\delta
\]

Within each group, heritability is by definition $h^2 = V_G/(V_G +
V_E)$, where $V_G$ and $V_E$ are the additive genetic and
environmental components of variance. The total variance within groups
can thus be written as $V_G + V_E = V_G h^{-2}$.  The additive genetic
variance contributed by a single locus within population~$i$ is
$2p_i(1-p_i)\alpha^2$, where $\alpha$ is the average effect of a gene
substitution \citep{Fisher:AE-11-53} and equals 1 for this model. For
either population, this equals $(1 - 4\delta^2)/2$. Summing across
loci,
\[
V_G = K(1 - 4\delta^2)/2
\]
The variance here is a sum of per-locus contributions because I have
assumed linkage equilibrium within each population between all pairs
of loci.

The mean difference between the character values of the two
populations, expressed in units of the within-population standard
deviation, is
\begin{eqnarray}
  \frac{D}{\sqrt{V_G + V_E}} &=&
  \frac{4K\delta}{\sqrt{h^{-2}K(1 - 4\delta^2)/2}}\nonumber\\
  &\approx& 5.7 h\sqrt{K}\delta
  \label{eq.reldiff}
\end{eqnarray}
where the approximation is good if $\delta^2$ is small. To interpret
this result, note that the right side is proportional to $\delta$,
which is itself proportional to $\sqrt{F_{ST}}$
(Eqn.~\ref{eq.Fst}). This shows that $F_{ST}$ does constrain the
difference between groups, as intuition would suggest. Yet if the
number of loci is large, this constraint is weak.

For example, suppose that $\delta=0.1$ so that $F_{ST} = 0.04$---a
value somewhat smaller than that of the continental human
populations. If heritability is $h^2=0.5$, and the number of loci is
$K=100$, then the two populations differ by 4 standard
deviations. Fig.~\ref{fig.4sigma} shows what this would look like. The
two distributions are well separated, with only minor overlap. If
instead there were 1000 loci, the difference would exceed 12 standard
deviations. Thus, group differences can be much larger than the
standard deviation, provided that $K$ is large.

This reflects the fact, mentioned above, that group differences scale
with $K$, whereas the within-group standard deviation scales with
$\sqrt{K}$. The same principle also operates in other contexts.  It is
why selection can move the mean of a quantitative character well
beyond the range of variation in the original population. It is also
why multilocus methods are better at classifying individuals into
groups than Lewontin's analysis \citep{Lewontin:EB-6-381} seemed to
suggest.  In both of these contexts, the theory leads to results
\parencites[pp.~218--219]{Falconer:IQG-96}[p.~799]{Edwards:BE-25-798}[p.~34]{Edge:SHP-52-32}
that are similar to my Eqn.~\ref{eq.reldiff}.

Under neutral evolution, the expected variance among groups in a
quantitative character is proportional to $F_{ST}$
\citep{Rogers+Harpending:QuantitativeCharacters}, and many studies
have compared observed variation to this neutral expectation. It
usually turns out that quantitative characters vary more than a
neutral model would predict, suggesting either natural selection or
environmental heterogeneity \citep{Leinonen:JEB-21-1}.  The present
results show that the genetic component of this excess may be very
large.  Small differences in allele frequencies can cause large
differences in a quantitative character.  Large group differences in a
quantitative character do not require large allele frequency
differences at underlying loci.
